The corpus used six strata:

1. a transparent current-DAW editorial frame plus publisher and product-hosted documentation;
2. early electrical, studio, synth, and broadcast material;
3. representative decade snapshots, supplemented by the repository's earlier 1980s and 1990s gap report;

For every accepted source, the inspection recorded terminology, abbreviations, visual topology, axis or UI context, gain polarity, medium, and communication method. ## Current production-tool synthesis

The editorial frame closes a bounded current-product check without claiming market share. Across the five numbered products, the reviewed publisher and product-ecosystem sources converge on the same response concepts but do not converge on one communication mechanism:

- Ableton, Image-Line, Apple, and Avid evidence already in the ledger combines response graphics, labels, abbreviations, controls, and compact selectors in product-local ways.
- REAPER EV-211 puts the type in a text dropdown and shows the instantiated response on a frequency/dB grid with parameter nodes.
- Bitwig EV-212 uses graph-edge gestures and changing mouse cursors to create types.
- Reason EV-213 uses rack-panel strip labels and a configured response display.

This is semantic convergence with representational diversity. A compact selector can be useful and recurring without being the only, oldest, or universally shared form.

## Evolutionary sequence: supported and unsupported claims

The corpus supports the following descriptive sequence across media:

1. circuit networks and prose define passed and attenuated frequency regions;
2. axis-bearing theoretical or measured response plots make the region shape visible;
3. labeled knobs, switches, and panel positions expose selectable response classes;
4. software combines text labels, parameter nodes, and live response graphs;
5. some products compress the familiar response topology into axis-free selectors or icon-font assets; and
6. public interchange still relies on prose, abbreviations, enums, semantic IDs, or images rather than a shared encoded response character.

The sequence is an inference from changing representational affordances, not a documented chain of copying. No reviewed source says that a modern selector was designed from Campbell, Baxandall, Oberheim, Avid, or a particular engineering textbook. Similar topology can arise from the same response semantics without direct graphic inheritance.

### Semantic continuity

Semantic continuity is strongest where sources define which frequency region passes, rejects, or receives shelf gain:

- Campbell EV-214 describes preassigned ranges transmitted with small attenuation and neighboring frequencies attenuated.
- Oberheim EV-216 puts bandpass, lowpass, notch, and highpass in one selectable synth control.
- MIT EV-219 teaches low-pass, high-pass, band-pass, and band-stop as four transfer-function classes.
- Current DAWs and manufacturers continue the same pass/affected-region distinctions through labels, parameters, and interactive graphs.

Terminology changes do not erase the underlying response class. They also do not justify unrestricted aliases. In particular, MIT's phrase `band rejection (notch) center frequency` occurs inside its Band-stop derivation. It does not prove that every source's Notch type is the broad Band-stop class. DA-020's source-local mapping or contrast rule remains controlling.

### Graphic continuity

Graphic continuity is weaker:

- Campbell's circuit diagrams and received-current/attenuation plots are not compact response selectors.
- Baxandall's shelf-like bass and treble families are explicit, labeled, signed response graphs rather than type icons.
- Oberheim communicates four types by knob position, abbreviations, and prose without establishing response glyphs.
- Avid and REAPER show contextual configured graphs with axes, scales, and controls.
- Existing compact-selector evidence uses product-local contours and contexts.

The response graph supplies enough familiar topology that compact-selector compression is a plausible project inference, but no reviewed source documents that design step. Exact contour inheritance and a stable cross-publisher graphic genealogy remain unproven.

## Shelf topology and polarity boundary

Baxandall EV-215 is an important historical precursor and specimen for shelf-like behavior, but it does not use low-shelf/high-shelf terminology or standalone shelf selectors, and this corpus proves no direct descent into modern designs. Its bass and treble lift/cut curves are mirrored around 0 dB. Avid EV-218 likewise explains that movement below zero deemphasizes and movement above zero emphasizes. REAPER EV-211 and Reason EV-213 make signed gain a separate parameter.

Together these sources reinforce, rather than relax, the registry boundary:

- low shelf and high shelf identify the affected frequency side;
- boost and cut are parameter states;
- an upward or downward instantiated curve can communicate current gain sign;
- a sign-agnostic type selector must not silently acquire a fixed boost or cut meaning.

An axis-bearing graph can therefore be a useful expanded explanatory alternative, but it is not semantically identical to a neutral standalone type glyph. The graph often carries gain, frequency, Q, slope, and current state beyond the response-class identity.

## Education, media, and community communication

MIT EV-219 uses formulas and axis-bearing tolerance diagrams. MusicRadar EV-220 teaches with ordinary terms, prose, and screenshots of configured vendor products. Sound Stack Exchange EV-221 shows practitioners asking about low-pass/high-cut equivalence in plain text.

These strata demonstrate repeated need to communicate the concepts, but they also provide adverse evidence to a universal character-necessity claim. Prose, abbreviations, mathematical notation, product screenshots, and contextual graphs frequently succeed. Embedded vendor screenshots in education or media are evidence of the article's teaching method; they are not independent adoption of the pictured vendor's graphic design.

## Geographic, language, and archive gaps

The corpus is heavily English-language and weighted toward the United States, United Kingdom, and Western European software ecosystems. Japanese-origin vendors are mostly represented through English manuals. A German-labeled Roland download inspected during research delivered an English effect appendix, so it was not counted as German terminology evidence.

Under-sampled regions and languages include Latin America, Africa, South Asia, the Middle East, and primary-language East Asian publication and user practice. The archive favors durable PDFs, manuals, patents, famous products, and surviving web pages. It under-samples handwritten studio notes, console recall sheets, local-language software builds, accessibility names, captions, messaging applications, forum images lost to link rot, unpublished engineering drawings, and everyday professional communication.

These gaps prevent claims of global prevalence, universal recognition, earliest use, or a complete evolutionary chain. The current editorial frame also prevents market-share claims.
